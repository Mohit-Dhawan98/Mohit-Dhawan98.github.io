Dating Environment

dating-env is a simulated dating world with a hidden answer key. You write a matching rule in a few lines, it proposes introductions for several weeks, and the world tells you how many couples it found out of the most that were possible.

Why it exists

The mutual-match case study describes a matching method that worked on a live app. A reader has to take that on trust. The production code and its data belong to a client and are not public.

This is the part that can be public. It is an open version that approximates the method from the article, written from scratch, with made-up people in place of real ones. It is not the production implementation and shares none of its code or data. It carries 12 structured features where the article describes 26, and its text and photo signals are plain vectors, not readings of real profiles.

How it works

A world is built from a seed and a set of assumptions you can argue about without reading code: how balanced the two sides are, how often people say yes, how much of attraction no system can observe. From its hidden truth the world works out how far every possible pair would get, in four stages: both say yes, the conversation keeps going, they meet, and both want to meet again.

A rule sees only what an app could have observed. People who form a couple leave. People who keep hearing nothing open the app less and eventually give up, so a rule that wastes attention early has fewer people to work with later.

What you can do with it

  • Write a rule and score it. The three stages from the article are included as rules of a few lines each, next to simpler baselines.
  • Change the world. Five preset worlds ship with it, and every setting states its meaning, its range and the basis of its default.
  • Run a tournament. Every rule against every world, across seeds, with the spread shown.
  • Check calibration. Compare predicted chances that both say yes with what happened, and fit the rank curve from logged decisions.

Status and known gaps

I am not quoting results from it here. A number from a simulation describes the world that was configured, and the repository itself shows no output for that reason.

  • It cannot confirm its own premise. The world assumes dealbreakers are real and that behavior reveals more than stated preference. A method built on those ideas is then tested where they hold.
  • The two decisions are treated as independent. Real decisions move together. The product of two chances is useful for ordering pairs and should be checked before it is trusted as a probability.
  • It does not scale. Every pair is scored in memory, because that is the clearest way to show the idea.
  • It is not stable matching. Assignment is greedy over mutual top pairs and makes no stability guarantee.

The principles behind it

The objective is two-sided: recommending one person to another is only useful if the second would choose the first. Dealbreakers are filters and not features, because a weighted score lets enough agreement elsewhere outvote the one disagreement that decides the outcome. And what people do counts for more than what they said at signup. The repository lists eleven of these, each pointing at the function that carries it.
